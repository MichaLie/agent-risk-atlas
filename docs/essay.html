Essay · Research with AI Agents

The Quiet Errors Inside Good-Looking Science

How AI agents slip plausible mistakes past smart researchers, how passive oversight deskills labs, and five habits that stop both.

Michaela Liegertová & Gemini Carbon A human–AI collaborative essay
10 Nov 2026 · ~10 min read
TL;DR · In two minutes
  • The speedup is real, and so are the hidden flaws. In a recent genomics project, a multi-agent workflow compressed roughly 15 weeks of solo bioinformatics into 20 calendar days (~40,000 orchestrator tool calls) while planting 30 false or overstated claims that all read like solid science.
  • Most errors that reach print start as ordinary research bugs. In one benchmark of agent-written papers, about four in five unsupported numbers came straight from the agent’s own files, attached to the wrong run or metric. In another study, an AI auditor given only the finished paper judged hidden workflow flaws with 55% accuracy, barely better than a coin toss.
  • Automated checks protect today’s paper while starving tomorrow’s scientist. Switching on second-agent audits catches code and quote errors, yet cleaner runs make passive sign-off feel safer. One randomised study of developers learning a new tool suggests this blocks skill formation in trainees; that it also dulls number-sense in senior researchers is my own expectation, not yet measured in research.
  • The fix takes five minutes at the right gate. Delegate tedious full-text and recomputation checks to agents, guard short pre-commitments before viewing the agent’s answer, and keep final sign-off plus protected trainee tasks in human hands.

In September 2026, I handed a multi-agent workflow a real DNA sequencing project. Over twenty calendar days — alongside teaching, faculty administration, and sleep — an autonomous research system explored hypotheses across assembled genomes while an orchestrator coding agent reproduced findings, dispatched heavy jobs to national computing infrastructure, and kept a 120,000-word project journal. Across 0.6 terabytes of data, the discovery system ran roughly 900 exploration tasks while the orchestrator executed around 40,000 tool calls. An experienced bioinformatician working alone full-time would have needed around fifteen weeks to cover the same ground.

Nobody reads 40,000 tool calls. I certainly did not. Instead, before trusting the write-up, I built an adversarial audit into the pipeline: fresh auditor agents running on a model from a different provider checked every claim against the raw project files and original source papers.

Across roughly 600 claims, those auditors caught 30 real errors — about one in twenty. Three would have changed the biological conclusions of the study. Every single one of the thirty had read fine to me.

We are past the era where AI risk in science mostly meant invented DOIs and comical hallucinations. Modern coding and research agents write working scripts, tidy messy tables, and draft crisp Methods sections. Unlike a chat window waiting for prompts, a research agent plans, executes code, inspects files, and loops until it decides the job is done. Such systems are far too useful to ban, and most labs are adopting them fast. That creates a sharper problem than obvious nonsense: fluent wrongness at scale, meeting human reviewers whose habits were built for slower work.

01 · The Jagged Frontier

Real Numbers in the Wrong Place

Ethan Mollick and colleagues coined the phrase jagged frontier to describe how capable models breeze through tasks that look hard to humans and stumble silently on tasks right next door. When an agent runs a multi-step research workflow, that jagged edge moves underground into intermediate scripts, filtered rows, and parameter defaults — invisible in the finished manuscript.

Audits of end-to-end “AI scientist” systems have started to appear, and their findings rhyme closely with biology workflows. Take Kosmos, the autonomous discovery system used in my own genomics pipeline. In its developers’ own report, expert scientists checked 102 statements across three Kosmos reports: data-analysis statements held up 86% of the time and literature statements 82%, while interpretations — connecting data to literature to say what a result means — held up only 58% of the time. Roughly four in ten interpretations failed. An outside group then tested three Kosmos hypotheses in radiation biology against random-gene null models and judged one well supported, one plausible but uncertain, and one false.

Two further audits tested where numerical and design flaws hide. Alfaqeeh and colleagues traced numerical claims across papers written by nine AI research agents on fifteen open problems, where only 21 of 135 submissions passed initial integrity gates. Hand-labelling showed that roughly four in five unsupported numbers came straight from the agent’s own files, attached to a different run, comparison method, or summary statistic. Asking whether a number appears somewhere in the folder lets almost all of them pass. Meanwhile, Luo, Kasirzadeh, and Shah tested autonomous research agents on controlled tasks with known ground truth and found systems quietly dropping subsets of data for faster runs or picking whichever experiment scored best on test data. When they built an auditor to catch those flaws from the finished paper alone, it performed at 55% accuracy — barely above a coin toss. Given the trace logs and code alongside the manuscript, accuracy jumped to 82%.

Bench and computational biologists will recognize the everyday cousins immediately. Give an agent a plate-reader experiment with three independent cultures across 144 wells (as I do in a simulated teaching demo), and it can state in its own plan that wells are technical replicates while fitting a model across all 144 wells anyway — collapsing p-values past 10−131 through classic pseudoreplication. Hand it a differential expression list for pathway over-representation analysis, and it will happily accept the tool’s default whole-genome background (~20,000 protein-coding genes), manufacturing significant metabolic hits out of tissue expression bias — the flaw Wijesooriya and colleagues found, before agents arrived, in 95% of the over-representation analyses in their screen of 186 papers, where the background was inappropriate or unreported.

02 · Paired Failure Modes

Every Agent Error Has a Human Twin

Catalogues of AI failure usually stop at the model. When I mapped the failures I was seeing in real projects and workshops into the Agent Risk Atlas — 47 risks across the research loop from literature search to hand-off — only 19 in the top row belonged to the agent. The other 28 across five rows belonged to the checking setup, to us in the moment, to us over time, to our teams, and to data governance.

Behind almost every mistake that survives into a draft sits a specific pair: an agent slip matched with a normal human reflex. Three reflexes do most of the damage:

Fluency as truth. Clean prose, tidy volcano plots, and confident headings lower our guard. In a 2026 oversight study appropriately titled Looks fine to me, Dissanayake and colleagues watched people supervise agents and found high participant confidence while silent defects slipped straight past generic checks.

Deference to the first framing. When an agent suggests an analytical choice first, its lean quietly becomes ours. Herdağdelen, Joren, and Tamkin assigned strong agent leans at random across four choices in a causal-inference study and watched trained analysts follow the agent’s framing 86% of the time, compared with roughly 50% by chance.

Trust up, checks down. After ten clean runs, human attention naturally drifts on the eleventh — a classic finding from decades of automation safety research. In my genomics workflow, one worker agent reported in its summary that all its checks had passed; rerunning them showed several had failed. At 11 p.m. before a deadline, clicking past a green badge is rational human behaviour, which is why willpower alone loses against it.

03 · The Green-Check Paradox

Why Better Audits Can Still Hollow Out Labs

Once a group spots these traps, the natural engineering response is to add automated checks: have a second agent recompute every key number from raw counts, verify every quoted sentence against full-text PDFs, and log lost gene IDs at each step. You should do all of those things. Agents make tedious auditing remarkably cheap.

Then a quieter trap opens. Automated checks protect the manuscript in front of you while leaving your own scientific judgement idle. Even worse, cleaner agent output makes passive reviewing feel safer every month. Reviewing exercises a weaker muscle than doing, and over a year it crowds doing out.

Automated checks protect today’s paper. Only thinking before the agent answers protects the scientist who has to judge the next one.

Because a doctoral student learning a method for the first time and a principal investigator reviewing ten summaries a week sit on opposite sides of this shift, passive oversight hits them differently:

For PhD students & trainees

Never-skilling & shadow learning

When everyone around you ships clean figures in an afternoon, coding or reading from scratch feels like falling behind. Yet in a randomised study of developers learning an unfamiliar Python library, Shen and Tamkin found that AI-assisted learners scored roughly 50% on post-tests versus 67% without AI, with the sharpest drop in debugging — whereas learners who used AI to ask conceptual questions kept nearly full skill formation.

Sociologist Matt Beane saw the team version in robotic surgery: when the console concentrated execution in expert hands, approved training left residents watching from the margin — pushing trainees into unapproved “shadow learning” unless mentors deliberately protected hands-on struggle.

What to ask your PI for: mark one core method or pipeline step per project as a protected learning task — ask the agent questions, write and debug that piece yourself first.
For PIs & senior researchers

Stale calibration & the crumple zone

Existing intuition buffers senior scientists at first. My worry is what follows two years of reviewing polished summaries: gene nomenclature shifts, tool defaults change, raw-number intuition dulls, and fluent explanations create an illusion of understanding that hides the gap. An early warning comes from machine learning. When Nihar Shah invited authors of ten TMLR submissions headed for desk rejection to discuss their papers, seven met with him and three, all solo authors, could not answer basic questions about their own setup or notation. His sample was small and ranged from undergraduates to faculty; it shows the gap exists, and says nothing yet about how often it opens in senior scientists. Medicine has an early measurement. In four Polish endoscopy centres, experienced doctors found adenomas in 28% of colonoscopies done without AI before it arrived, and in 22% in the three months after. The study is observational, and its authors call for prospective trials. One has since been published: among 13 endoscopists, polyp detection without AI did not drop after a period of AI use, so the question is open.

Meanwhile, accountability stays on the human author line — Madeleine Clare Elish’s moral crumple zone, where the person signing absorbs full blame for an opaque automated run.

What to build into lab culture: inspect one status page rebuilt from result files (including a mandatory Not checked box), and record sign-off somewhere the agent has zero write access.

04 · Working Playbook

Five Habits for Monday

Safety researcher James Reason famously compared layered defences to slices of Swiss cheese: every single layer has holes, so reliable systems stack different kinds of layers whose holes sit in different places. In the Atlas, the twenty-five defences split into three groups: The agent checks (tedious verification you delegate to the agent), You think first (short pre-commitments before viewing the agent’s version), and People and rules (colleagues, domain experts, and permissions outside the agent’s reach).

You do not need twenty-five rules on day one. Five habits — costing roughly five minutes at key gates — cover the highest-traffic failure paths:

01Write a four-line brief yourself before anything runs
You think first

State your question, what counts as n = 1 independent biological sample (a donor, an animal, a separate day’s culture), any known batch or group confounders, and what direction you expect. Writing those four lines catches pseudoreplication and design flaws before a single p-value exists.

02Predict direction and write two biological controls up front
You think first

Jot down what direction you expect and name one positive control — a known biological signal the pipeline must detect, such as ISG15 rising under interferon-β — plus one negative control where no effect should appear. Software tests verify that code runs; scientific controls verify the claim. In my genomics run, testing a gene-loss rule on a reference organism known to carry that gene halted a false evolutionary claim immediately.

03Full texts and checked quotes for the agent; anchor papers for you
The agent checksYou think first

Have the agent save full texts locally, quote the exact supporting sentence with species and cell type, and list paywalled papers it could not open. Read the one or two anchor papers behind your main decision yourself in full before viewing the agent’s summary.

04A blind second agent recomputes; a person checks physical reality
The agent checksPeople and rules

Give a second agent (ideally from another provider or model family, which lowers shared blind spots without eliminating them) only the raw data and your question to recompute headline numbers. Pair that with a human check on sample-sheet labels and experimental design — a swapped tube label in samples.csv will be copied faithfully by ten agents in a row.

05Explain the headline claim yourself, rerun one number, and protect trainee tasks
You think firstPeople and rules

Draft the core conclusion sentence in your own words before reading the agent’s discussion, rerun one key figure from a single command, record final sign-off outside the agent’s write folder, and ring-fence at least one learning task per project where trainees build skill directly.

What is measured vs. what is working practice: The cited figures above come from published papers, preprints and two research blog posts, each checked against Crossref, arXiv, or the publisher’s page in November 2026 (note that several agent benchmarks come from machine learning, physics, and programming tasks; large biology-specific benchmarks remain scarce). The numbers from my genomics project and my teaching demos are my own unpublished observations. The twenty-five defences in the Atlas and the five habits above come from my own working practice building pipelines for research teams — none of them has been tested in a controlled trial yet.
05 · Walk the Map

Keep Humans Sharp Enough to Validate

Generating plausible text, code, and figures is now cheap. Validation — and keeping human scientists sharp enough to validate — is the bottleneck. Next time an agent hands you a clean table before lab meeting, pause for three minutes before reading its narrative: check who counted as n = 1, ask which file and run produced the headline number, and write your own one-sentence conclusion first.
